Uncertainty LHS Tab

The Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) tab is part of FracSTAT's Uncertainty Analyzer, a self-contained probabilistic workspace for evaluating fault and fracture stability under uncertain stress, pore-pressure, rock-strength, and orientation conditions. Unlike a deterministic calculation that returns one value for each input condition, the LHS workflow propagates uncertainty through the Mohr–Coulomb fault-stability calculation and produces probability distributions for Slip Tendency (Ts), Dilation Tendency (Td), Fracture Susceptibility (Sf), and Coulomb Failure Index (Fsi).

Module status and implementation: The LHS interface is implemented as a thin dispatcher in uncertainty_lhs_tab.py. It creates the shared sampling workspace from uncertainty_sampling_tab.py with the sampling method fixed to "lhs". The LHS and Monte Carlo tabs therefore share the same controls, analysis modules, plots, caching, validation and export infrastructure; the principal difference is how the underlying sample coordinates are generated.

1. Purpose and Scope

The LHS module provides a stratified, variance-reduced probabilistic simulation environment for evaluating mechanical stability of faults and fractures. It propagates uncertainty in the three principal stresses, pore pressure, cohesion, friction, SHmax azimuth, strike and dip through the same Mohr–Coulomb mechanics used by the deterministic stability calculator.

The Uncertainty Analyzer is independent of the Static and Dynamic Analyzer workspaces. Its input tables, sampling settings, cached results and session state are private to the uncertainty workspace and are not written back into the Static/Dynamic shared project cache.

1.1 What the LHS module provides

  • Stratified Latin Hypercube sampling with reproducible random seeds.
  • Uncertain stress, strength, pressure and orientation inputs.
  • Mohr–Coulomb fault-stability calculations for every realization.
  • Probability distributions for Ts, Td, Sf and Fsi.
  • Descriptive statistics, convergence diagnostics and analytical/reference validation.
  • SRC and PRCC sensitivity analysis.
  • Spatial probability mapping when coordinate data are supplied.
  • Publication-oriented visualization, image export and tabular result export.

2. Interface Overview

The LHS tab occupies one page of the CTkTabview created by UncertaintyAnalyzerApp._build_workspace_tabs. It contains the same workspace structure as the Monte Carlo tab, but uses sampling_method="lhs".

The status area reports loading results, simulation outcomes, convergence state, cancellation and export locations. SVG loading and scale calibration are disabled in this workspace because the uncertainty module works with tabular fault data rather than digitized fracture traces.

Each sampling tab contains six nested modules in a fixed order: Geomechanics, Statistics, Sensitivity, Visualization, Spatial Probability Map, and Validation & Export. Only the currently visible module is redrawn after a run or fault switch, which keeps large ensembles responsive.

5. Mathematical Background

5.1 Latin Hypercube Sampling design

For dimensionality d = 9 and sample size N, LHS partitions the [0,1] probability range of each dimension into N equal-probability strata. Exactly one sample is taken from every stratum in each dimension, while the stratum indices are independently permuted between dimensions (McKay et al., 1979). FracSTAT generates the design using scipy.stats.qmc.LatinHypercube(d, seed) (Virtanen et al., 2020).

This produces evenly stratified one-dimensional projections and generally reduces the variance of estimated output statistics, especially quantiles, compared with simple pseudo-random Monte Carlo sampling of the same size (McKay et al., 1979); Helton and Davis (2003) document the same behaviour for propagation through complex system models. The design is a plain Latin hypercube: the strata are permuted independently and no space-filling criterion is optimised over candidate permutations (cf. Morris and Mitchell, 1995), and no rank-correlation structure is induced across dimensions (cf. Iman and Conover, 1982).

Why use LHS? LHS is particularly useful when a simulation must estimate stable distributions or tail statistics with a limited computational budget. Every marginal input range receives deliberate coverage rather than relying on random draws to cover it evenly.

5.2 Probability distributions

DistributionParameterisationTypical use
Truncated NormalRequested moments (mean, SD) plus physical bounds [a,b]; the parent normal's μ, σ are solved forBounded stress/strength inputs.

Physical bounds are enforced: friction is restricted to [0,1], cohesion and pore pressure are non-negative, and principal stresses are constructed so that all realizations remain non-negative. When a bounded quantity is assigned Normal/Gaussian, the sampler silently uses the equivalent Truncated Normal law and records the effective distribution in reproducibility metadata.

The values entered are the moments of the law that is actually sampled. SciPy's truncnorm takes loc/scale as the parent normal's μ and σ, and the truncated distribution's own mean and standard deviation differ from those whenever a bound is within reach (Johnson, Kotz and Balakrishnan, 1994, Vol. 1, sec. 13.10):

formula
α = (a − μ)/σ,   β = (b − μ)/σ,   Z = Φ(β) − Φ(α)

E[X]   = μ + σ · (φ(α) − φ(β)) / Z

Var[X] = σ² · [ 1 + (α·φ(α) − β·φ(β))/Z − ((φ(α) − φ(β))/Z)² ]

distributions._truncated_normal_parent therefore solves these two relations for the parent (μ, σ) whose truncation reproduces the requested mean and SD, as nested bracketed root finds: E[X] is monotone in μ, and the realised SD is then monotone in σ. Both roots are verified before use.

What passing the moments through directly did: a cohesion entered as mean 2.0 MPa, SD 3.0 MPa with a lower bound of 0 was actually sampled with mean 3.2821 MPa, SD 2.1891 MPa — the mean 64% above the value typed into the table. The bias grows as the requested mean approaches a bound relative to the spread, so the most tightly bounded inputs (cohesion and pore pressure near zero, friction near 0 or 1) were the worst affected.

A bounded support cannot carry every (mean, SD) pair: on [0, ∞) the ratio SD/mean has a supremum of 1 (the exponential limit), on a finite [a,b] the SD cannot exceed (b − a)/√12 (the uniform limit), and both ceilings tighten as the mean approaches a bound. An unattainable request raises an error naming the largest SD that support can carry and suggesting a Lognormal, Gamma or Uniform law, rather than quietly sampling different moments.

5.3 Orientation sampling

Strike and SHmax are circular/azimuthal quantities, so a linear normal distribution is unsuitable near the 0°/360° wrap-around. FracSTAT uses the Von Mises distribution (Mardia and Jupp, 2000, ch. 3), where concentration k controls clustering: k = 0 is uniform and larger k produces tighter concentration around the mean.

Dip is bounded to 0–90° and is sampled from a bounded Truncated Normal distribution whose standard deviation is derived from the concentration parameter. Alternatively, Fisher 3D couples Strike and Dip through a single spherical orientation distribution using Strike's concentration parameter (Fisher, 1953).

SHmax always uses its own independent Von Mises concentration because the maximum horizontal stress direction is an independent geomechanical measurement rather than a structural attribute.

5.4 Principal-stress ordering and regime remapping

The sampler guarantees σ1 > σ2 > σ3 by drawing σ3 directly and constructing σ2 and σ1 from σ3 plus two non-negative stress gaps. The configured stress distributions are retained while the gap construction prevents principal-stress crossing. The three regimes follow the Andersonian classification, in which one principal stress is vertical because the free surface carries no shear traction (Anderson, 1951); see also Zoback (2007, ch. 1) for the modern stress-state formulation.

RegimeVertical stressCalculation remap
Normal Faultingσ1True σ1 is placed in the vertical slot.
Strike-Slipσ2True σ2 is placed in the vertical slot.
Reverse/Thrustσ3No remap; sampled ordering already matches the calculator convention.
The remap permutes inputs, not geometry. calculate_fault_stability hard-codes one geometric assignment — its first argument onto the horizontal axis parallel to SHmax, its second onto the other horizontal axis, its third onto the vertical — and does not infer a regime. Inside the calculator the argument names therefore denote slots, not the global maximum and minimum; only the reverse/thrust case preserves both identities. The displayed and exported ensemble always keeps the sampled σ1 > σ2 > σ3 columns, and an unrecognised regime string falls back to reverse/thrust. Because only squared direction cosines enter σn and τ, the permutation changes those scalars by exactly the intended amount and by nothing else; the regime attribute is recorded on results.attrs["stress_regime"] for reproducibility.

5.5 Fault-stability equations

For every realization, the downward-directed unit normal (pole) of the fault plane is constructed from strike and dip in an (East, North, Up) frame under the right-hand rule — dip direction = strike + 90°, so the pole trends strike − 90° and plunges 90° − dip — and then projected onto the horizontal principal axes defined by the SHmax azimuth:

formula
n         = ( −sin(dip)·cos(strike),  sin(dip)·sin(strike),  −cos(dip) )

rotated_x = nx·sin(SHmax) + ny·cos(SHmax)        (component along SHmax)
rotated_y = nx·cos(SHmax) − ny·sin(SHmax)        (component along the other horizontal axis)

σn        = σ1·rotated_x² + σ2·rotated_y² + σ3·nz²
|T|²      = σ1²·rotated_x² + σ2²·rotated_y² + σ3²·nz²
τ         = √ max(|T|² − σn², 0)
Why the signs matter even though the scalars did not change. The projection uses the fact that a unit vector at azimuth A is (sin A, cos A) in (East, North). The previously used pair — with the sine and cosine of the strike exchanged in n, and the two horizontal projections swapped — describes a left-hand-rule plane whose pole trends −strike, and projects onto azimuth 90° − SHmax. It reproduces σn, τ and Ts to within 2.3 × 10−15, because only squared direction cosines enter those scalars and one principal axis is vertical. Every signed use of the normal — the pole stereonet, the slip direction, the rake — was nevertheless wrong, which is why the convention is fixed here and independently cross-checked against a full stress tensor (Section 8.4).
MetricFormulaInterpretation
Slip Tendency (Ts)Ts = τ / σusedRatio of resolved shear to normal stress; a plane is expected to slip as Ts approaches μ (Morris et al., 1996).
Dilation Tendency (Td)Td = (σ1used − σused) / (σ1used − σ3used)Normalized tendency for extensional dilation/opening (Ferrill and Morris, 2003). Evaluated from the unpermuted sampled σ1 and σ3 (both reduced by Pp when the effective-stress switch is on), so it is a property of the stress state rather than of the calculator's slot assignment.
Fracture Susceptibility (Sf)Sf = σ′n − (τ − C0) / μ = (σn − Pp) − (τ − C0) / μThe sustainable pore-pressure increase before the fault reaches the Coulomb criterion, in MPa (Mildren et al., 2002; Streit and Hillis, 2004). Always on effective normal stress — see below.
Coulomb Failure Index (Fsi)Fsi = τ / (C0 + μσused)Fsi ≥ 1 indicates predicted shear failure on the linear Mohr–Coulomb envelope (Jaeger et al., 2007, ch. 4).

Effective normal stress is σ′n = σn − Pp, the Terzaghi effective-stress form (Terzaghi, 1943) as applied to faulting by Hubbert and Rubey (1959). The Effective-Stress switch determines whether total or effective stress is used for Ts, Td and Fsi.

Sf is exempt from the switch. Sf answers "how much more pore pressure can this fault take?", a question that only exists under the effective-stress principle (Terzaghi, 1943; Hubbert and Rubey, 1959), and its standard definition is on σ′n (Mildren, Hillis and Kaldi, 2002; Streit and Hillis, 2004). There is no total-stress form of it. When Sf was allowed to follow the switch, the default total-stress mode dropped Pp entirely and the reported margin became statistically independent of pore pressure — measured correlation −0.005168 with an SRC of −0.0005, i.e. the one control an operator can actually manipulate registered as having no influence on the metric designed to constrain it. Sf is undefined (NaN) when σ′n ≤ 0 or μ ≤ 0.

The failure flag is Sf ≤ 0, equivalent to the Fsi ≥ 1 criterion, but it is stored as a three-valued boolean: True, False, or missing where Sf could not be evaluated:

python
result["failure"] = pd.array(
    np.where(np.isfinite(sf_values), sf_values <= 0, None), dtype="boolean"
)
Why the third state is necessary. In IEEE-754 arithmetic np.nan <= 0 is False, so a plain comparison silently reclassifies every non-evaluable realization — tensile effective normal stress, zero or negative friction — as "did not fail". Probability of failure, computed as a mean over the flag, is then biased downwards: the unsafe direction. With the nullable dtype those rows are excluded from the denominator instead of counted as successes.

5.6 Sensitivity coefficients: SRC and PRCC

SRC is obtained from ordinary least-squares regression of standardized target output against standardized inputs. PRCC measures rank correlation after the effect of all other inputs has been removed in rank space (Iman and Helton, 1988; Marino et al., 2008). Both range from −1 to +1; magnitude indicates relative influence and sign indicates direction.

Two preparation steps are applied to the design matrix before either coefficient is computed. Strike is folded modulo 180°, because it is axial and the artificial discontinuity at 180°/360° would otherwise enter the regression as if it were real variation in orientation. Multi-fault population runs are collapsed to one row per realization by aggregate_paired_faults, so the ranking represents the population rather than one fault's individual response.

Why the collapse is not cosmetic. Every fault in a realization is evaluated on the same sampled stress state, so the nine input columns repeat identically within a realization and only the target varies across its faults. Fitting the un-aggregated rows replicates each input vector nfaults times and folds purely within-realization scatter — variation no input can explain, since the inputs are constant across it — into a model whose unit of observation is really the realization, inflating the apparent sample size and deflating R² (Efron and Tibshirani, 1993, ch. 8; Field and Welsh, 2007). The sidebar therefore reports paired samples (realizations), not rows.

Squared SRCs partition the output variance — that is, they satisfy ΣSRC² = R² and may be read as variance shares — only when the inputs are mutually uncorrelated and the response is linear in them (Iman and Helton, 1988; Saltelli et al., 2008, ch. 1; Marino et al., 2008, sec. 2). Neither condition is guaranteed here: the ordered-gap construction of Section 5.4 can draw σ1, σ2 and σ3 with strong mutual correlation. The module therefore reports three diagnostics with every run — model R², ΣSRC², and the largest |r| between inputs — and adds a warning when |r| > 0.1 and |ΣSRC² − R²| > 0.05 (read the SRCs as conditional regression weights and rank with PRCC instead), or when R² < 0.6 (the response is not well described by a linear model).

The measured case this guards against. On a correlated-stress run the pairwise input correlations reached 0.7977, 0.6027 and 0.7529, and ΣSRC² reached 1.6294 against an R² of 0.9513 — the squared coefficients summed to 171% of the variance they were being read as dividing up. With none of the three numbers displayed, that tornado chart was indistinguishable on screen from a valid variance decomposition.

5.7 Descriptive and confidence-interval statistics

The Statistics module reports mean, sample standard deviation, median, mode, P10, P50 and P90. The 95% confidence interval of the mean is approximated as:

formula
CI95% = x̄ ± 1.96 × (s / √n)

Strike (period 180°, axial) and the SHmax azimuth (period 360°, directional) wrap, so neither has a meaningful arithmetic mean or standard deviation. When one of them is the displayed variable, the location and spread are computed on the circle instead — the mean resultant vector of the angles mapped onto the full circle, with mean = atan2(S, C) and s.d. = √(−2·ln R), scaled back by period/360° (Mardia and Jupp, 2000, sec. 2.2.1, 2.3.2 and 2.3.4; Fisher, 1993, sec. 2.3.1). The P10/mean/P90 overlay on those two inputs is likewise taken on the angular residual about the circular mean, and a band that straddles the wrap point is drawn as the two arcs it actually occupies. Measured on a von Mises sample centred on a true strike of 0.04°, the linear arithmetic mean read 89.89° — the perpendicular orientation; an SHmax azimuth concentrated on 359.98° read 180.28°. Dip is a plunge confined to 0–90° and does not wrap, so it keeps the linear summary.

The s/√n interval assumes independent, identically distributed rows. Neither holds exactly for an LHS ensemble pooled across faults: the design is deliberately stratified rather than independent (which is what makes it more efficient than plain Monte Carlo), and in a multi-fault run every fault of a realization shares one stress state, so rows come in correlated clusters. The card interval is therefore a quick indication of precision, not a calibrated bound. Where a standard error is used to make a decision — the convergence test of Section 8.4 — it comes from a bootstrap that resamples whole realizations rather than from this formula.

7. Internal Workflow

7.1 End-to-end run sequence

  1. Read and validate SAMPLES and SEED.
  2. Assemble means, standard deviations, distributions, bounds, orientation concentrations, effective-stress setting and stress regime.
  3. Snapshot the selected fault or the complete fault table for an All-Faults run.
  4. Disable run/input controls and display the progress overlay.
  5. Run sampling_engine.generate_samples and validation in a daemon worker thread.
  6. Return progress updates to the Qt main thread through app.after(0, ...).
  7. On success, cache results, refresh the visible module, mark the workspace dirty, autosave and report effective-distribution substitutions.
  8. On cancellation, restore the idle interface while preserving previous cached results.
  9. On other exceptions, restore the interface and show the error message.

7.2 Threading and responsiveness

File loading and simulation are performed on background threads. Qt UI updates are routed through app.after(0, ...), keeping window dragging, tab switching and cancellation responsive during long simulations.

7.3 Progress reporting

Progress is reported in coarse stages rather than once per realization. The progress workload includes distribution preparation, sampling and stability calculation for each fault, followed by convergence, analytical benchmark, reference comparison and metadata stages.

7.4 Cancellation

A threading.Event is checked at fault-level checkpoints inside generate_samples. Cancellation is therefore cooperative and normally becomes effective within roughly one fault's worth of computation.

7.5 Fault-selection caching and effective-stress toggle

Completed results are cached using the active fault-selection label. An existing All-Faults result can be filtered for an individual fault without redundant simulation. The effective-stress switch recalculates Ts, Td, Sf, Fsi and the failure flag for active and cached results without sampling again.

7.6 Autosave

Loading data, switching modes, changing the active module and completing a simulation trigger silent autosave through core.project_state.save_state. Uncertainty session state remains independent from Static and Dynamic project state.

8. Statistical Methods and Validation

8.1 Descriptive statistics

Statistics are recalculated whenever the selected metric, input variable or plot type changes. They always use the currently selected fault's cached or freshly computed results.

8.2 Sensitivity analysis

SRC and PRCC populate the numeric sensitivity table and tornado chart. The tornado can be sorted by ascending or descending absolute magnitude or left in input-declaration order. The sidebar note beneath the table always carries the three diagnostics of Section 5.6 — model R², ΣSRC², and the largest |r| between inputs — together with the number of paired samples the fit used.

8.3 Fault-level quantile envelopes

per_fault_quantiles groups realizations by fault ID and calculates P10, P50 and P90. These values define the uncertainty band and central-tendency line used by the Visualization module.

8.4 Validation diagnostics

CheckMethodPass criterion
Nested convergenceProgressively larger nested subsets are evaluated for Ts mean/P50/P90, Sf mean and failure probability. Each checkpoint carries a bootstrap standard error alongside its estimate.Both conditions, per metric: precision — 1.96 × SEM at the largest level within 2% of the final value (1 percentage point for failure probability); and stability — the last two successive increments both within the same tolerance. Fewer than two levels, or a non-finite SEM, fails.
Analytical benchmarksPrincipal-axis edge cases are checked against analytically expected values, including Sf on effective normal stress.Absolute tolerance 1×10−10.
Independent tensor referenceVectorized results are compared with a separate per-row implementation that builds the full stress tensor σ = R·diag(σ1,σ2,σ3)·RT, forms the traction t = σ·n, and takes σn = n·t and τ = ‖t − σn·n‖.Maximum absolute difference ≤1×10−10.

Why an increment is not an error bound. The nested levels form a geometric ladder, so a single small step between two neighbouring sizes says only that the estimate happened to move little there. On heavy-tailed synthetic ensembles (Pareto Ts, normal Sf, 40 runs per size) the old final-increment-only rule "passed" 10 of 40 runs at 512 realizations, and 8 of those 10 were still more than 2% away from a 400,000-realization reference; the true remaining error reached 1.9× the increment that had been inspected. The precision condition supplies the missing piece — a Monte Carlo standard error is the actual measure of sampling precision (Helton and Davis, 2003; Saltelli et al., 2008, ch. 2) — while the two-increment stability requirement is the standard successive-refinement check (Roache, 1998, ch. 5).

The standard error resamples realizations, not rows. In a multi-fault run every fault of a realization shares one sampled stress state, so the rows are clustered and an ordinary row bootstrap would treat nfaults correlated values as independent evidence and understate the SEM. _cluster_bootstrap_standard_errors resamples whole realizations with replacement instead (Efron and Tibshirani, 1993, ch. 8; Field and Welsh, 2007), using 200 resamples, reduced to 60 above 20,000 rows for runtime; a checkpoint with fewer than two realizations returns NaN. In a single-fault run it reduces to the ordinary bootstrap.

The reference is deliberately built a different way. The vectorized path uses squared direction cosines, and that shortcut is exactly what concealed the pole-convention error of Section 5.5; a reference sharing the same algebra could not have detected it. The tensor path also computes Sf unconditionally on effective normal stress, matching the calculator. When the reference instead followed the effective-stress switch, reference_comparison(use_effective_ts=False) reported a maximum absolute error of 12.0 MPa and "passed": False on a correctly working calculator. The analytical benchmarks (σ1 = 60, σ2 = 40, σ3 = 25 MPa, Pp = 10, μ = 0.6, C0 = 0) expect Sf = 15 MPa for a horizontal plane and Sf = 50 MPa for the vertical case; the previously expected 25 and 60 MPa were the values the buggy calculator produced with Pp dropped, so the benchmark had been confirming the defect.

Interpretation of convergence: a passed convergence check indicates numerical stability and adequate sampling precision of the five reported summary statistics at the selected sample size. It does not extend to every downstream statistic — an extreme tail percentile or a per-fault probability may need considerably more realizations — and it does not prove that the assumed input distributions, means, standard deviations or physical model are correct.

12. Practical Examples

12.1 Population-level reactivation screening

  1. Load the fault orientation workbook with Load Excel/CSV.
  2. Set the Geomechanics means, SDs and distributions for σ1, σ2, σ3, friction, cohesion, pore pressure and SHmax.
  3. Leave Strike/Dip means blank when each fault's loaded orientation should be used.
  4. Select All Faults, choose a sample size such as 5,000, and run LHS.
  5. Check the Validation & Export convergence card. Increase SAMPLES and repeat if convergence fails.
  6. Use Visualization → Fault-Level Uncertainty Envelopes to inspect Fsi bands and the Fsi = 1 threshold.
  7. Load coordinates and use Spatial Probability Map to view P(Fsi ≥ 1) spatially.

12.2 Sensitivity-driven data acquisition

  1. Select a specific fault and run the LHS sampler.
  2. Open Sensitivity and choose Ts as the target.
  3. Use SRC or PRCC to rank the nine inputs.
  4. Identify the largest-magnitude sensitivity bar as the strongest contributor to Ts variability.
  5. Prioritize tighter field/laboratory measurement or a reduced uncertainty range for that input in a follow-up run.

12.3 Comparing total and effective stress

  1. Run with the Effective-Stress switch enabled and record the Fsi distribution.
  2. Disable the switch. Cached results are recalculated without re-sampling.
  3. Compare the two Fsi histograms to quantify the effect of pore-pressure assumptions on predicted failure probability.

13. Geological Interpretation

The four response metrics are Mohr–Coulomb fault-mechanics indicators used in reservoir geomechanics, induced-seismicity assessment and structural-diagenesis studies.

13.1 Slip Tendency (Ts)

Ts is the ratio of resolved shear stress to normal stress (Morris et al., 1996). A plane reaches frictional sliding when Ts approaches the static friction coefficient μ. Values around or above approximately 0.6 are commonly treated as near-critical in cohesionless applications, following Byerlee's experimental μ ≈ 0.6–0.85 for most rock types (Byerlee, 1978), but the actual interpretation should follow the friction and cohesion assumptions used in the simulation.

13.2 Dilation Tendency (Td)

Td identifies planes favourably oriented to open under the applied stress state (Ferrill and Morris, 2003). High Td can indicate fracture orientations that are more favourable for dilation and fluid-flow pathways.

13.3 Fracture Susceptibility (Sf)

Sf is expressed in MPa and is read as the pore-pressure increase the fault can sustain before the Coulomb criterion is met at the resolved shear stress (Mildren et al., 2002; Streit and Hillis, 2004): an Sf of 4 MPa means roughly 4 MPa of additional pressure would be expected to reactivate it. Positive Sf indicates a stable margin; Sf ≤ 0 indicates predicted shear failure at current conditions. Because it is a pressure margin it is always computed on effective normal stress, whatever the Effective-Stress switch is set to, and it is reported as missing rather than as a number where it cannot be evaluated (σ′n ≤ 0, or μ ≤ 0).

13.4 Coulomb Failure Index (Fsi)

Fsi is the ratio of resolved shear stress to Mohr–Coulomb frictional strength. Fsi = 1 lies on the failure envelope and Fsi ≥ 1 predicts shear failure. It is the principal metric used by the module's population-level fault-behaviour classification and spatial risk mapping.

13.5 Stress regime and orientation uncertainty

The stress-regime selector follows Andersonian normal, strike-slip and reverse faulting environments. Orientation concentration k provides a way to represent tight or diffuse structural clustering: higher k means a tighter orientation distribution, while lower k represents greater uncertainty. Fisher 3D provides a coupled strike-dip representation where appropriate.

13.6 Why uncertainty quantification matters

Deterministic stability calculations use single best-estimate values and can conceal measurement and interpretive uncertainty. LHS propagates those uncertainties through the same Mohr–Coulomb physics and produces probability distributions, allowing statements such as the probability that a particular fault is critically stressed under the assumed uncertainty model.

15. Limitations

  • The stability model assumes planar faults with one strike/dip and does not model curvature, roughness or heterogeneous strength along a single fault.
  • Cohesion, friction and pore pressure are spatially uniform per fault or population; depth-dependent and spatially varying property models are not implemented.
  • Convergence checks combine a bootstrap precision bound with a two-increment stability test on five named metrics (Section 8.4); they are a practical stopping rule for those statistics, not a formal confidence statement about every derived quantity.
  • SRC/PRCC can under-represent strongly non-monotonic input-output relationships, and squared SRCs are a variance decomposition only when the inputs are uncorrelated and the response is linear — read the reported R², ΣSRC² and largest input |r| alongside the tornado chart.
  • Fracture susceptibility is always computed on effective normal stress and is exempt from the Effective-Stress switch, because a sustainable pore-pressure increase has no total-stress form; it is undefined where σ′n ≤ 0 or μ ≤ 0, and those realizations are excluded from the failure probability rather than counted as non-failures.
  • Stress regimes are realised by permuting which sampled magnitude enters each slot of a fixed geometry (Section 5.4), not by re-deriving the geometry; an unrecognised regime string falls back to reverse/thrust.
  • A truncated normal cannot reach every (mean, SD) pair on a bounded support — the SD ceiling is (b − a)/√12 on a finite range and SD/mean < 1 on [0, ∞) — so a wide spread on a tightly bounded input must be expressed with a Lognormal, Gamma or Uniform law.
  • The ±1.96·s/√n interval on the statistics cards assumes independent, identically distributed rows, which a stratified LHS design pooled across faults does not satisfy; treat it as indicative and use the convergence diagnostics for decisions.
  • Circular treatment covers the mean, dispersion and overlay bands of strike and SHmax and the folding of strike before sensitivity fitting; formal circular-uniformity tests (Rayleigh, Watson) on the sampled orientations are not computed.
  • Fisher 3D uses one concentration parameter and cannot represent anisotropic elliptical orientation dispersion.
  • Spatial mapping requires exact fault-ID matching; fuzzy or partial ID matching is not performed automatically.
  • Computation is single-machine and single-process; practical ensemble size is limited by available memory and Matplotlib/Qt rendering responsiveness.

Latin hypercube propagation of stress, strength and orientation uncertainty through the Mohr–Coulomb fault-stability model (Jaeger et al., 2007), with N realizations per fault drawn from a nine-dimensional stratified design (McKay et al., 1979) generated by scipy.stats.qmc.LatinHypercube under integer seed s (SciPy; Virtanen et al., 2020) and mapped to the stated marginal distributions by inverse CDF; Normal selections on bounded inputs are replaced by moment-matched truncated normals (Johnson et al., 1994), and fault orientations are sampled either from independent von Mises laws (Mardia and Jupp, 2000) or from a coupled Fisher spherical distribution (Fisher, 1953). Sensitivity is reported as standardized regression and partial rank correlation coefficients (Iman and Conover, 1982; Iman and Helton, 1988; Saltelli et al., 2008) computed on realization-aggregated rows, and convergence as a bootstrap precision bound (Efron and Tibshirani, 1993) (Helton and Davis, 2003) combined with a successive-refinement stability test (Roache, 1998). Analyses were run in Python with NumPy (Harris et al., 2020), pandas (McKinney, 2010) and Matplotlib (Hunter, 2007); the exported metadata sheet records the exact versions, seed and input hash.
